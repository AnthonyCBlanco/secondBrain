### Idea
An **Electric Dipole** is a system consisting of two equal but opposite point charges ($+q$ and $-q$) separated by a very small distance $d$. 

Even though the total net charge of a dipole is zero, the spatial separation of the charges means they don't perfectly cancel each other out in the space around them. The **Electric Dipole Moment** is a vector that characterizes the strength and orientation of this system. It is a vital concept for understanding how molecules (like water) interact, and how dielectric materials behave in capacitors.

### Formally
The electric dipole moment $\vec{p}$ is defined as the magnitude of one of the charges multiplied by the distance vector between them:
$$
\vec{p} = q \vec{d}
$$
- **Magnitude**: $p = qd$
- **Direction**: In physics, the dipole moment vector points **from the negative charge to the positive charge**. *(Note: Chemists often draw this arrow in the exact opposite direction, but the physics math requires $- \to +$).*

### Dipoles in an External Electric Field
When you drop an electric dipole into an external uniform [[Electric Fields|Electric Field]] ($\vec{E}$), two things happen:

1. **Zero Net Force**: Because the charges are equal and opposite, the force on the positive end ($+q\vec{E}$) perfectly cancels the force on the negative end ($-q\vec{E}$). The dipole does not translate (drift) through space.
2. **Torque (Twisting)**: While it doesn't drift, the opposing forces on opposite ends cause the dipole to spin. The field exerts a **torque** that tries to force the dipole moment $\vec{p}$ to perfectly align with the electric field $\vec{E}$.
   $$ \vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E} $$
   $$ \text{Magnitude: } \tau = pE \sin(\theta) $$
   *(Where $\theta$ is the angle between the dipole moment and the electric field).*

### Potential Energy of a Dipole
Because the electric field exerts a torque to twist the dipole into alignment, forcing it out of alignment requires work. This means a misaligned dipole stores **Potential Energy ($U$)**:
$$
U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E} = -pE \cos(\theta)
$$
- **Minimum Energy (Most Stable)**: Occurs when $\theta = 0^\circ$ (the dipole is perfectly aligned with the field). $U = -pE$.
- **Maximum Energy (Most Unstable)**: Occurs when $\theta = 180^\circ$ (the dipole is perfectly anti-aligned, pointing directly against the field). $U = +pE$.

### The Field Generated BY a Dipole
If you are far away from a dipole ($r \gg d$), the positive and negative charges *almost* cancel each other out, but not quite. While the field of a single point charge drops off according to the inverse-square law ($1/r^2$), the electric field of a dipole drops off much faster, proportional to **$1/r^3$**.
