### Idea
While [[Coulomb's Law]] calculates the direct force between two specific charged particles, the concept of an **Electric Field** allows us to look at a single charge and understand how it alters the space around it. 

An electric field is a [[Vector Fields|vector field]] that permeates space. It tells us exactly what force *would* be exerted on a hypothetical "test charge" if we were to drop it at any specific coordinate in that space.

### Formally
The electric field $\vec{E}$ at a given point in space is defined as the electrostatic force $\vec{F}$ that would be exerted on a small, positive test charge $q_0$ placed at that point, divided by the magnitude of the test charge:
$$
\vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0}
$$
*(Because of this, Electric Fields are measured in **Newtons per Coulomb (N/C)** or Volts per meter (V/m)).*

By substituting Coulomb's Law into this definition, the electric field generated by a single point charge $q$ at a distance $r$ is:
$$
\vec{E} = k \frac{q}{r^2} \hat{r}
$$

### Electric Field Lines (Visualization)
We map out electric fields using continuous arrows called field lines. They follow strict geometric rules:
1. **Direction**: Field lines always point **away from positive** charges and **towards negative** charges. (Because they represent the force on a *positive* test charge).
2. **Strength**: The closer together the lines are packed, the stronger the electric field is in that region.
3. **No Crossing**: Electric field lines never cross each other. If they did, it would mean a single test charge would be pushed in two different directions simultaneously, which violates vector addition.

### The Superposition Principle
Just like the force in Coulomb's Law, electric fields obey the Principle of Superposition. If you have a cluster of multiple point charges, the net electric field at any empty point in space is simply the **vector sum** of the individual electric fields generated by each charge.
$$ \vec{E}_{net} = \vec{E}_1 + \vec{E}_2 + \dots + \vec{E}_n $$

### Example
**What is the magnitude of the electric field $2.0 \text{ m}$ away from a point charge of $5.0 \mu\text{C}$?**

1. Convert microcoulombs to coulombs: $q = 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ C}$.
2. Use the point charge formula:
   $$ E = k \frac{|q|}{r^2} $$
   $$ E = (8.99 \times 10^9) \frac{5.0 \times 10^{-6}}{2.0^2} $$
   $$ E = \frac{44,950}{4} = 11,237.5 \text{ N/C} $$

If you placed a positive $1 \text{ C}$ charge at that exact spot, it would experience $11,237.5 \text{ N}$ of repulsive force.
