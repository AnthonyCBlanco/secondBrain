### Idea
A **nuclear reaction** involves a change in an atom's nucleus, often resulting in the transformation of one element into another (transmutation).  
This is fundamentally different from a chemical reaction, which only involves the rearrangement of electrons and bonds, leaving the nuclei unchanged.

### Formally
**Radioactive decay** occurs when an unstable nucleus emits particles and/or electromagnetic radiation to become more stable.

The types of radioactive decay include:
1. **Alpha ($\alpha$) Decay:** Emits an alpha particle ($\ce{^4_2He}$). Mass number decreases by 4; Atomic number decreases by 2.
2. **Beta ($\beta^-$) Decay:** Emits a beta particle ($\ce{^0_{-1}e}$). Mass number unchanged; Atomic number increases by 1.
3. **Positron ($\beta^+$) Emission:** Emits a positron ($\ce{^0_1e}$). Mass number unchanged; Atomic number decreases by 1.
4. **Electron Capture (EC):** Nucleus captures an inner-shell electron. Mass number unchanged; Atomic number decreases by 1.
5. **Gamma ($\gamma$) Emission:** Emits high-energy electromagnetic radiation ($\ce{^0_0\gamma}$). No change in mass or atomic number.

### Nuclear vs. Chemical Reactions
| Feature | Nuclear Reactions | Chemical Reactions |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Particles Involved** | Protons, neutrons, electrons, and other subatomic particles. | Valence electrons. |
| **Transmutation** | Elements can be converted into other elements. | Elements remain unchanged. |
| **Energy Changes** | Huge amounts of energy released or absorbed ($10^8$ kJ/mol). | Relatively small amounts of energy ($10^1$ to $10^3$ kJ/mol). |
| **Reaction Rates** | Not affected by temperature, pressure, or catalysts. | Affected by temperature, pressure, concentration, and catalysts. |

### Example
**Write the balanced nuclear equation for the alpha decay of Uranium-238.**

$$
\ce{^{238}_{92}U -> ^{234}_{90}Th + ^4_2He}
$$

Notice that both the **mass numbers** ($238 = 234 + 4$) and **atomic numbers** ($92 = 90 + 2$) are conserved.

### Nuclear Stability and Kinetics
The stability of a nucleus depends primarily on the **neutron-to-proton ratio (N/Z)**. 
- For atomic numbers $Z \le 20$, $N/Z \approx 1$. 
- For $Z > 20$, $N/Z > 1$. 
- Any element with $Z > 83$ is inherently radioactive.
- Nuclei with **magic numbers** of protons or neutrons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) are exceptionally stable.

Radioactive decay is a **first-order kinetic process**:
- **Rate Law:** Rate $= \lambda N$
- **Half-Life:** $t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda}$

### Mass Defect and Binding Energy
The **mass defect ($\Delta m$)** is the difference between the mass of an isotope and its constituent protons and neutrons.  
**Nuclear Binding Energy** is the energy required to break a nucleus apart, calculated using Einstein's equation:

$$
\Delta E = (\Delta m)c^2
$$
