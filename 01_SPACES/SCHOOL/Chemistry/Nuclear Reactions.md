## Introduction
A **nuclear reaction** involves a change in an atom's nucleus, often resulting in the transformation of one element into another (transmutation). This is fundamentally different from a chemical reaction, which only involves the rearrangement of electrons and bonds, leaving the nuclei unchanged.

### Nuclear vs. Chemical Reactions
| Feature | Nuclear Reactions | Chemical Reactions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Particles Involved** | Protons, neutrons, electrons, and other subatomic particles. | Valence electrons. |
| **Transmutation** | Elements can be converted into other elements. | Elements remain unchanged. |
| **Energy Changes** | Huge amounts of energy released or absorbed ($10^8$ kJ/mol). | Relatively small amounts of energy ($10^1$ to $10^3$ kJ/mol). |
| **Reaction Rates** | Not affected by temperature, pressure, or catalysts. | Affected by temperature, pressure, concentration, and catalysts. |

## Types of Radioactive Decay
Radioactive decay occurs when an unstable nucleus emits particles and/or electromagnetic radiation to become more stable.

1. **Alpha ($\alpha$) Decay**
   - **Particle emitted:** Alpha particle ($\ce{^4_2He}$)
   - **Characteristics:** Heavy, low penetrating power (stopped by paper).
   - **Effect:** Mass number decreases by 4; Atomic number decreases by 2.
   - **Example:** $\ce{^{238}_{92}U -> ^{234}_{90}Th + ^4_2He}$

2. **Beta ($\beta^-$) Decay**
   - **Particle emitted:** Beta particle (electron, $\ce{^0_{-1}e}$ or $\beta^-$)
   - **Characteristics:** A neutron is converted into a proton and an electron. Moderate penetrating power.
   - **Effect:** Mass number remains unchanged; Atomic number increases by 1.
   - **Example:** $\ce{^{14}_6C -> ^{14}_7N + ^0_{-1}e}$

3. **Positron ($\beta^+$) Emission**
   - **Particle emitted:** Positron ($\ce{^0_1e}$ or $\beta^+$)
   - **Characteristics:** A proton is converted into a neutron and a positron.
   - **Effect:** Mass number remains unchanged; Atomic number decreases by 1.
   - **Example:** $\ce{^{11}_6C -> ^{11}_5B + ^0_1e}$

4. **Electron Capture (EC)**
   - **Process:** The nucleus captures an inner-shell electron, converting a proton into a neutron.
   - **Effect:** Mass number remains unchanged; Atomic number decreases by 1 (same net effect as positron emission).
   - **Example:** $\ce{^{81}_{37}Rb + ^0_{-1}e -> ^{81}_{36}Kr}$

5. **Gamma ($\gamma$) Emission**
   - **Particle emitted:** Gamma ray ($\ce{^0_0\gamma}$), which is high-energy electromagnetic radiation.
   - **Characteristics:** Often accompanies other types of decay. High penetrating power (requires thick lead/concrete to stop).
   - **Effect:** No change in mass number or atomic number. Just a transition to a lower energy state.

## Balancing Nuclear Equations
In a balanced nuclear equation, two key quantities must be conserved:
1. **Conservation of Mass Number:** The sum of mass numbers (superscripts) on the reactant side must equal the sum on the product side.
2. **Conservation of Atomic Number:** The sum of atomic numbers (subscripts) on the reactant side must equal the sum on the product side.

## Nuclear Stability
Not all nuclei are stable. The stability of a nucleus depends primarily on the **neutron-to-proton ratio (N/Z)**.
- **Low Atomic Numbers ($Z \le 20$):** Stable nuclei typically have an N/Z ratio $\approx 1$.
- **Higher Atomic Numbers ($Z > 20$):** Stable nuclei require an N/Z ratio $> 1$ (up to about 1.5) to counteract the electrostatic repulsion between protons.
- **Band of Stability:** A plot of neutrons vs. protons for stable isotopes. Nuclei outside this band are radioactive.
- **Magic Numbers:** Nuclei with certain numbers of protons or neutrons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) are exceptionally stable, similar to noble gas electron configurations.
- Any element with $Z > 83$ (Bismuth) is inherently radioactive.

## Kinetics of Radioactive Decay
Radioactive decay is a **first-order kinetic process**.
- **Rate Law:** Rate $= \lambda N$, where $\lambda$ is the decay constant and $N$ is the number of radioactive nuclei.
- **Integrated Rate Law:** $\ln\left(\frac{N_t}{N_0}\right) = -\lambda t$
- **Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$):** The time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay.
  $$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$$
  *Note: Unlike chemical reactions, the half-life of a radioactive isotope is constant and independent of the initial concentration.*

## Nuclear Fission and Fusion
- **Nuclear Fission:** A heavy nucleus (like Uranium-235) splits into two or more lighter, more stable nuclei, releasing a large amount of energy and additional neutrons (which can trigger a chain reaction).
- **Nuclear Fusion:** Two light nuclei (like isotopes of Hydrogen) combine to form a heavier, more stable nucleus. This releases even more energy per gram than fission and is the process that powers the sun and stars. Requires extremely high temperatures to overcome electrostatic repulsion.

## Mass Defect and Binding Energy
- **Mass Defect ($\Delta m$):** The mass of a nucleus is always *less* than the sum of the masses of its individual protons and neutrons. This "missing mass" is the mass defect.
- **Nuclear Binding Energy:** The energy required to break a nucleus into its component protons and neutrons. It is derived from the mass defect using Einstein's equation:
  $$\Delta E = (\Delta m)c^2$$
  *(Where $c = 3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s. Usually calculated in MeV or Joules/nucleon to compare stability).*
